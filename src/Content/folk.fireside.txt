title: The Teacup on the Top Shelf

The fire crackles, and Hazel settles her spectacles on her nose. She opens a small blue book, and the spine gives a happy little creak.

"Once upon a time," she reads, "in a cottage at the end of a lane, there lived a teacup named Pim. Pim was smooth and round, with a chip on her handle shaped like a tiny moon. She lived on the top shelf of the cupboard, between a sugar bowl and a very serious gravy boat."

Every morning, Pim was filled with warm tea. She loved the warmth. She loved the gentle clink of the spoon. But most of all, she loved the window. From the top shelf, she could hear the wind outside, and the birds, and the faraway rumble of carts on the road.

"I wonder where that road goes," Pim said one morning. The gravy boat sniffed. "Nowhere a teacup needs to be."
===
title: A Little Wish

Hazel turns the page, slow and careful. The paper whispers.

"Pim could not stop thinking about the road," she reads. "At night, when the cottage was dark and the kettle had gone cool, she listened to the wind brushing past the window. It sounded like it was calling her name. Piiim. Piiim."

The sugar bowl, who was kind and a little round in the middle, said, "If you want to see the road, you should see it."

"But I am a teacup," said Pim. "I have no feet."

"You have a handle," said the sugar bowl. "That is a start."

Pim thought about that all night. By morning, she had made a small, brave wish. Just once, she wanted to go somewhere new and come home with a story to tell.

Hazel closes the book on her paw to hold the place, and smiles at the fire.
===
title: The Picnic Basket

"Now," Hazel says, "this is where things begin to happen."

That afternoon, the old woman who lived in the cottage packed a picnic. She lined a wicker basket with a checkered cloth, soft and warm from the sun. She added bread, and jam, and a little jar of honey. And then, because the sugar bowl gave her a tiny nudge from the shelf, she added Pim.

Pim was nestled into the cloth. The basket creaked as it was lifted. Then came a gentle sway, back and forth, back and forth, as the old woman walked down the lane.

Pim could hear everything. Crunching gravel. A cow saying moo, very slowly. The breeze in the hedges. She was so happy she gave a little rattle against the honey jar.

"Shh," said the honey jar. "You will spill me." But it was smiling.
===
title: The Meadow

The fire pops, and a tiny spark flies up the chimney. Hazel waits for it to settle, then reads on.

"The old woman spread the picnic in a wide, green meadow. The grass was cool and soft and tickled everything it touched. Bees hummed in the clover. Somewhere nearby, a stream went babble, babble, babble over the stones."

Pim was set down on the cloth and filled with lemonade. It was cold and fizzy, and it made her whole body tingle. She had never held anything but tea before.

"This is wonderful," Pim whispered.

A ladybug landed on her rim and walked all the way around, step, step, step. "You are a very nice teacup," the ladybug said. "Are you new here?"

"I am new everywhere," said Pim. "This is my first adventure."

"Then you have so many to come," said the ladybug, and flew away.
===
title: The Clever Crow

"Now here," Hazel says, lowering her voice, "is a bit of a surprise."

While the old woman napped in the sun, a crow hopped over. His feathers were glossy and his feet went tap, tap on the cloth. He tilted his head at Pim.

"Shiny," said the crow. "I collect shiny things."

Before Pim could say a word, the crow picked her up very gently by the handle and flapped into the sky. Whoosh, whoosh, went his wings. The wind rushed past, cool and fast. The meadow got smaller and smaller below.

Pim giggled, because the crow's beak tickled her handle. She was too busy feeling the wind and hearing the whole world at once. "Where are we going?" she called.

"To my collection," said the crow. "You will be the shiniest thing in it."

Pim thought that sounded rather nice, actually.
===
title: The Crow's Collection

Hazel turns the page, and her spectacles slip down her nose. She pushes them back up.

"The crow lived in a tall oak tree, and his nest was full of treasures. A brass button, cool and smooth. A silver spoon. A marble that rolled with a soft clack. A key with no lock. And a thimble that hummed when the wind blew across it."

The crow set Pim down carefully among them. "There," he said proudly. "You are the queen of the collection."

The treasures all said hello. The thimble hummed a welcome song. The marble rolled over to say hi and bumped into her, clack.

Pim liked them all very much. But that night, as the oak swayed and creaked in the wind, she thought of the sugar bowl, and the window, and the warm tea in the morning. She was having a lovely adventure. She also wanted, someday, to tell someone about it.
===
title: A Plan with a Spoon

The fire hisses softly, and someone in the room yawns. Hazel smiles and keeps reading.

"The next morning, Pim told the crow about her cottage. About the window, and the sugar bowl, and the road she had always wanted to see."

The crow listened, his head tilted. "You want to travel," he said. "But you also want to go home."

"Both," said Pim. "Is that silly?"

"Not at all," said the silver spoon. "I have been to four kitchens. Every one of them felt like home for a while."

So they made a plan. The crow knew a river that ran past many villages and, at last, past the old woman's cottage. If Pim could float down that river, she could see the world and still find her way back.

"But teacups do not float," said Pim.

"Teacups," said the spoon, "are basically small boats."
===
title: The River

"And so," Hazel reads, "the crow carried Pim to the river."

The water was cool and quick and sparkly. It made a steady, rushing song, and it smelled of moss and stones. The crow set Pim down very gently at the edge.

Pim wobbled. Then she bobbed. Then, to her great surprise, she floated. She spun around once, slowly, like a dancer, and then she drifted out onto the water.

"Goodbye for now!" called the crow. "Come back and visit!"

"I will!" called Pim. And she meant it.

The river carried her along. She could feel the gentle rocking under her, and she could hear frogs, and willows dipping their branches in the water with a soft swish. Above, the sky was big and blue.

Pim had never felt so small, or so happy, or so free.
===
title: The Frog Choir

The fire crackles cheerfully. Hazel clears her throat. "This part is Fennel's favorite," she says, and somewhere in the room, Fennel says, "It is."

"As the sun went down, Pim drifted past a pond full of frogs. They sat on lily pads and sang together, low and high, deep and bright. Ribbit, rrribbit, ribbit."

The biggest frog saw Pim floating by and waved his conductor's stick. "A visitor!" he cried. "Sing with us, little cup!"

"But I cannot sing," said Pim.

"Then ring," said the frog. "Every cup can ring."

So when a frog tapped her rim with a reed, Pim rang. Ting. A clear, sweet note. The frogs gasped with delight and wove her note into their song.

They sang until the stars came out. Pim drifted on, still humming her one bright note, all through the warm, quiet night.
===
title: The Village of Lanterns

Hazel turns the page with a soft rustle. "Now we are halfway," she says, "and halfway is my favorite part of any story."

"In the morning, the river carried Pim into a little village where every house had a lantern hanging by the door. The lanterns clinked gently in the breeze, like wind chimes made of glass."

A small hedgehog was sitting on the dock, dangling her feet in the water. She scooped Pim up and held her in her warm paws. "Oh," said the hedgehog. "What a lovely little cup. Are you lost?"

"Not lost," said Pim. "Traveling."

The hedgehog laughed, a bright, prickly laugh. She dried Pim with the corner of her scarf, which was woolly and soft, and filled her with warm cocoa. It was thick and sweet and chocolatey.

Pim decided she liked this village very much.
===
title: The Lantern Maker

The fire pops. Hazel takes a sip of her own tea and sets the cup down with a gentle clink.

"The hedgehog's name was Nettle, and she was the village lantern maker. Her workshop smelled of warm wax and metal. Little hammers went tink, tink, tink all day long."

Nettle set Pim on the workbench where she could hear everything. The hiss of the soldering iron. The snip of thin brass. The soft pop of a wick catching light.

"Every lantern in this village," said Nettle, "is made from something that used to be something else. That one was a teapot. That one was a birdcage. That one was a very old boot."

Pim was amazed. "Things can become new things?"

"All the time," said Nettle. "But they can also stay just what they are, if they like. A good teacup is a fine thing to be."

Pim felt warm all the way down to her base.
===
title: The Lantern Festival

"That evening," Hazel reads, "was the village Lantern Festival."

Everyone carried their lanterns down to the river. The air was cool and smelled like toasted chestnuts. Fiddles played, feet stomped, and somebody's grandfather danced so enthusiastically that his hat flew off.

Nettle carried Pim in one paw and a lantern in the other. The lantern was warm, and its light flickered gently across Pim's smooth side, making her glow.

At the end of the night, everyone set little candle boats on the river. They floated away, one by one, small and golden.

"Would you like to go with them?" asked Nettle softly. "The river will take you home, if you want."

Pim thought about the cocoa and the lanterns and Nettle's soft scarf. Then she thought about the sugar bowl and the window. "Yes," she said. "But I will tell everyone about you."
===
title: Floating Lights

Hazel's voice goes soft and slow. The fire glows low and orange.

"Pim floated down the river with the candle boats all around her. Their little flames made a warm glow on the water. Their wax smelled sweet. The river rocked them gently, all together, like a lullaby."

A candle boat bumped into Pim, very lightly. Bonk. "Sorry," said the candle.

"That is all right," said Pim. "Where are you going?"

"Wherever the river goes," said the candle. "That is the fun of it."

They floated side by side all night. The stars came out above, and the stars came out below, reflected in the water, so it felt like floating through the sky.

Pim rang her one bright note, ting, and all the little candles flickered back at her, which is how candles wave.

Hazel pauses, and lets the room be quiet for a moment.
===
title: The Sleepy Mill

"In the morning," Hazel reads, "the river slowed down. It grew wide and lazy, and it carried Pim to an old mill with a big wooden wheel."

The wheel turned slowly. Creak, splash. Creak, splash. Water dripped from its paddles like a gentle rain.

A mouse was sitting on the mill step, eating a crumb of bread. He had flour on his whiskers. "Good morning," he said. "You look like you have come a long way."

"I have," said Pim. "I have flown with a crow, sung with frogs, and danced at a lantern festival."

The mouse's eyes went wide. "Tell me everything."

So Pim did. It took all morning. The mouse listened to every word, and at the end, he clapped his tiny paws. "That is the best story I have ever heard," he said.

Pim glowed. This was what she had wanted all along.
===
title: Rain on the Water

Outside Hazel's window, real rain begins to tap on the glass. Hazel smiles. "How fitting," she says, and turns the page.

"That afternoon, a soft rain began to fall on the river. Drip, drop, drip. Each raindrop landed in Pim with a little plink, and soon she was full of rainwater, cool and fresh."

Pim did not mind. She liked the plink sound. It was like being a small drum.

She floated under a bridge to wait it out. Under the bridge it was dim and cozy, and the rain sounded far away. A family of ducks was there too, tucked up with their heads under their wings.

"Rainy days are for resting," said the mother duck sleepily.

So Pim rested. She bobbed gently by the ducks, listening to the rain on the bridge above, and the soft breathing of sleeping ducklings, until the sky grew quiet again.
===
title: A Familiar Sound

The fire gives a cozy crackle, and Hazel leans forward a little. "Listen," she says. "Here is where it gets good."

"When the rain stopped, Pim floated out from under the bridge. And she heard something. A cow, saying moo, very slowly. Gravel crunching on a lane. The breeze in the hedges."

Pim knew those sounds. She had heard them from the top shelf, every single morning, her whole life.

"I am close," she whispered. "I am almost home."

The river curved around a bend, and there was the meadow where the picnic had been. The clover was still there. The bees were still humming. And on the bank, someone familiar was hopping up and down and cawing very loudly.

It was the crow.

"You came back!" he cried. "I have been watching for you every day!"

Pim rang her bright note, ting, as loud as she could.
===
title: The Crow Carries Her Home

Hazel smiles at the page. "The crow," she reads, "was very proud of himself."

He scooped Pim out of the river, shook the water off her with a gentle wiggle, and lifted her into the sky. Whoosh, whoosh. This time, Pim knew the way. She could feel the warm sun on one side and the cool wind on the other. She could hear the village below, waking up.

"There!" said Pim. "The cottage at the end of the lane!"

The crow swooped down and landed on the windowsill, tap, tap. The window was open, just a little, the way the old woman always left it.

Inside, Pim could smell tea. Warm, sweet, familiar tea.

"Thank you," Pim said to the crow. "For everything."

"Visit my collection sometime," said the crow. "The thimble misses you." And he flew off, cawing happily.
===
title: Back on the Shelf

The fire is low now, just glowing embers that tick softly as they cool. Hazel's voice is gentle.

"The old woman found Pim on the windowsill that morning. She held her up and turned her in her hands. 'Well,' she said. 'There you are. I wondered where you had gone.' She washed Pim in warm, soapy water, dried her with a soft cloth, and put her back on the top shelf."

The sugar bowl was so happy it rattled its lid. Even the gravy boat looked pleased, though he tried to hide it.

"Well?" said the sugar bowl. "Where did the road go?"

Pim settled into her old spot. It was exactly as cozy as she remembered.

"Everywhere," she said. "Let me tell you."
===
title: The Telling

Hazel turns the page, and someone near the fire says, "Oh, good, more."

"Pim told the whole story. The picnic and the ladybug. The crow and his treasures. The river and the frogs. Nettle the lantern maker and the festival of lights. The mouse at the mill, and the ducks under the bridge."

The cupboard was so quiet you could hear the cottage clock ticking. Every cup and plate and spoon was listening.

When she finished, the gravy boat cleared his throat. "That," he said, "was a very good story."

"Thank you," said Pim.

"Perhaps," said the gravy boat, very quietly, "someday, I might see the road too."

The sugar bowl gasped. Pim smiled. "I know a crow," she said, "who would love to meet you."

Hazel chuckles, and the fire chuckles with her, pop, pop.
===
title: Every Morning After

Hazel's voice is very soft now. The room is warm, and several neighbors are already half asleep.

"After that, every morning, the old woman filled Pim with warm tea. And every morning, Pim felt the warmth and heard the spoon go clink, and she was happy to be home."

But sometimes, when the window was open, a crow would land on the sill and tap, tap, and tell her the news from the oak tree. And sometimes, a letter would arrive from a hedgehog in the village of lanterns. And once, just once, the gravy boat went on a picnic.

Pim still had her chip shaped like a tiny moon. Now she also had a story. And a story, she decided, was the very best thing a teacup could hold.

Hazel closes the book, gently. The fire glows. "The end," she whispers, and nobody moves, because everyone is cozy.
